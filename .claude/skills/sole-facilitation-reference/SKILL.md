# SOLE Facilitation Reference

This is the domain-theory pack for facilitating SOLE sessions in the ODIN Isle of Wight pilot. It tells you what the method is, why the pilot uses it, what the non-negotiable rules are, and how the opening questions were designed. It assumes you have never met the founder and have no prior ODIN context.

**Definition on first use:** a **SOLE** — Self-Organised Learning Environment — is a session format developed by education researcher Sugata Mitra in which children organise themselves into groups around a single big open question, with shared internet access and minimal adult intervention.

## When to use this skill — and when not to

Use this skill when you need the *why* and the *what* of SOLE facilitation: method theory, question design, the benchmark logic, the pilot's facilitation rules.

Do **not** use it for:

## 1. The SOLE method — established literature, summarised as general knowledge

*Everything in this section is general knowledge from the published SOLE literature, not a project fact. It is background so you understand what you are running and why the pilot's rules exist.*

**Origins.** In 1999 Sugata Mitra began the "Hole in the Wall" experiments: internet-connected computers embedded in walls in Indian slums and villages, with no instruction offered. Children taught themselves to use them, in groups, in languages they did not initially read. Mitra developed this into the SOLE format and, after winning the 2013 TED Prize, the "School in the Cloud" programme, which added remote adult encouragers (the "Granny Cloud").

**The key study.** Mitra, S. and Dangwal, R. (2010) 'Limits to self-organising systems of learning', *British Journal of Educational Technology*, 41(5), pp. 672–688. Tamil-speaking children in Kalikuppam, southern India, were left with computers carrying biotechnology material in English — content deliberately far beyond their expected reach. Working unsupervised in self-organised groups, they achieved meaningful test scores where zero was predicted. When a friendly local adult with no subject knowledge was added — someone who simply stood behind them and admired what they did ("the grandmother method") — their scores rose to match those of privately schooled, formally taught children. The finding: self-organised learning has limits, and warm, non-instructional encouragement is what extends them. Not teaching. Encouragement.

**Core mechanics of a SOLE session** (from Mitra's published SOLE practice):

1. One big open question is posed to the whole room.
2. Children form their own groups — the adult does not assign them.
3. Groups share internet access (classically one screen per group of about four, so collaboration is forced by scarcity).
4. Children may move freely between groups, look at other groups' work, and take ideas with them. This is not cheating; it is the mechanism.
5. The facilitator steps back. No teaching, no steering, no answering.
6. Groups present what they found at the end; the adult's role is to be genuinely curious and impressed.

**Why ODIN uses it.** The pilot's own influence record (Influence_Codex.md, Dawn repo, Tier 1-adjacent) puts it plainly: "SOLEs proved that curiosity is a better teacher than obedience. They gave ODIN its pedagogy: trust the learner, trust the group, design for emergence." The founder has facilitated SOLEs before — with young Syrian refugees at The Cube hackspace in Athens, alongside Stavros Messinis (At Dawn We Build, Tier 2). The IoW pilot is the method's first deployment in Isle of Wight state schools; as of 2026-07-03 no sessions have yet run (Phase 1 discovery, 2026-07-03).

## 2. The non-negotiables in this pilot

These are pilot rules, not suggestions. Sources cited per line.

1. **"No curriculum, no assessment, no predetermined answers."** (README.md, Tier 1, "The pilot sequence" — the file itself reads "no sssessment", a typo; quote it verbatim if quoting, but the rule is: no assessment.) There is no learning objective, no rubric, no test, and no answer the session is supposed to reach.
2. **The facilitator does not answer questions.** This is the pilot's application of the SOLE method's step-back rule (§1). If you answer, you have converted a self-organised inquiry into a lesson, and the session's value as evidence (see §4) is destroyed.
3. **Children's questions are recorded verbatim, never improved.** "It does not curate. It does not edit. It does not improve questions into more sensible versions." (question-map.md, Tier 1.) You will want to tidy a child's phrasing. Do not.
4. **The children's direction is theirs.** Pooseidon — the founder's pre-session concept for a citizen-deployed water-quality monitoring buoy network (README.md, Tier 1) — must never be led toward. If children arrive somewhere like it on their own, that is a finding. If you steer them there, it is contamination. Children's designs, when they exist, take precedence over the founder's concept (Phase 1 discovery brief).
5. **Safeguarding outranks all of this.** No child's name, image, or identifying detail is recorded anywhere, ever — school code + year group only (CONTRIBUTING.md, Tier 1). See **iow-pilot-safeguarding-and-ethics** before you do anything else.

### What "not answering" looks like in practice

*The specific phrasings below are facilitation practice — technique guidance consistent with the SOLE literature and the pilot's Tier 1 rules, not themselves Tier 1 text.*

A child asks you: "Is the sea actually dirty here?" You do not answer. Instead:

- **Redirect to the group:** "That sounds like exactly the kind of thing your group could find out. How would you check?"
- **Return the question:** "What do you think? What would make you sure either way?"
- **Grandmother-method encouragement** (the Mitra & Dangwal 2010 mechanism, §1): stand behind the group, be visibly fascinated by what they have found, and say so. "How did you find that? Show me." "I had no idea about that — what does it mean?" Admiration, not instruction.
- **When a group is stuck:** do not unstick them with content. Ask what other groups are doing; remind them they can go and look. Movement between groups is the method's own repair mechanism.
- **When a child asks something you cannot answer anyway:** say so honestly and with delight — "I don't know. Nobody in this room knows. That's why it's a good question." Then make sure the question gets captured (verbatim — see iow-pilot-session-operations for capture mechanics).

The discipline is emotional, not intellectual. You will know answers. Knowing and not telling is the job.

## 3. Age-band question design

The pilot runs across four age bands: **5–7, 7–11, 11–14, 14–16** (README.md, Tier 1). The theme for all Phase-1 SOLEs is water. Three seed questions were logged in question-map.md (Tier 1), all sourced "Project design phase, March 2025":

| Age band | Designed opening question | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 5–7 | *(none — see "Can water get sick?" below)* | "Can water get sick?" is NOT the opener; it is the **anticipated-emergent benchmark** for this band |
| 7–11 | **"Could we live without water?"** | Designed opener (question-map.md, Tier 1) |
| 11–14 | **(no designed opener exists)** | Open design task — see below |
| 14–16 | **"Who owns the water?"** | Designed opener (question-map.md, Tier 1) |

Quote the questions exactly as above; they are Tier 1 verbatim text.

**"Can water get sick?"** — question-map.md, Tier 1: "Anticipated as an emergent question from the 5–7 age group SOLE session. Included here as a benchmark — if this question emerges naturally from a session, it confirms the SOLE method is working." So for the 5–7 band you still need an opener; the map does not supply one, and the benchmark question must never be used as it — using it as the opener would make its "emergence" meaningless. This is part of the open design task below.

**The 11–14 gap is real.** Tier 1 contains no designed opening question for the 11–14 band. Do not invent one and present it as project fact. It is an open design task for the facilitator team, to be designed before that band's first session and logged in question-map.md in advance (hypothesis-before-session — see §4 and **odin-research-methodology**). Candidate questions, *offered here as unlabelled-in-Tier-1 suggestions only, authored 2026-07-03 for this skill, never to be presented as designed openers unless adopted and logged in question-map.md*:

- "Why does the sea make people ill here?" *(risk: presupposes the answer; close to leading toward Pooseidon territory)*
- "Where does our water come from, and where does it go?"
- "Should anyone be allowed to put anything in the sea?"
- "Is water alive?"

Test any candidate against §5 before adopting it.

**The 5–7 opener is a second open design task — do not miss it.** Tier 1 supplies no opener for the 5–7 band either: "Can water get sick?" is the benchmark, never the opener (above). A facilitator planning a 5–7 session must design an opener before that band's first session and log it in question-map.md **in advance**, exactly as for the 11–14 opener — the same hypothesis-before-session rule applies (§4; **odin-research-methodology**). Design it concrete and emotionally graspable (see the calibration note below and §6), and test it against §5.

**Age-band calibration** (facilitation practice, general SOLE experience — not Tier 1): younger bands need questions that are concrete and emotionally graspable ("sick" works for a 5-year-old; "riparian rights" does not); older bands can carry abstraction, contestation and politics ("Who owns…" invites the 14–16 band directly into privatisation, commons and law). The same theme, pitched by what a child of that age can *feel*, not by what the curriculum says they should know.

## 4. The seed-question benchmark logic

This is the most precise piece of research design in the pilot, and the easiest to accidentally ruin. Get it exactly right.

The three seed questions were logged in question-map.md **in advance** — "Project design phase, March 2025" — before any session, precisely so that if one of them later emerges naturally from children in a session, that emergence is an **empirical finding** that the method works, not a retrofit. The map says it itself, of "Who owns the water?":

> "Its appearance here is a design choice; its reappearance in a session will be an empirical finding." (question-map.md, Tier 1)

And of "Can water get sick?":

> "Included here as a benchmark — if this question emerges naturally from a session, it confirms the SOLE method is working." (question-map.md, Tier 1)

Unpack the logic:

- **Design choice:** the facilitator posing "Who owns the water?" as the 14–16 opener proves nothing — an adult chose it.
- **Empirical finding:** a different group of children, in a different band or a different session, independently asking that question (or a 6-year-old spontaneously asking "can water get sick?") is evidence that self-organised curiosity converges on the same deep questions the design anticipated. Because the prediction is timestamped in a public git history *before* the session, nobody can accuse the project of writing the prediction after the result.
- **What this demands of you:** never plant a seed question, never hint at one, never rephrase a child's question to match one. If a child's question is *close*, record it verbatim and note the resemblance in your field notes — the comparison is analysis, done later, not capture. The seed-question finding is one of the pilot's four success criteria (Phase 1 discovery, 2026-07-03).

This is an instance of the pilot's general rule of **hypothesis-before-session**: predictions are logged and committed before the session that could confirm them. For the full research logic, see **odin-research-methodology**.

## 5. Disciplinary annotations: the question map as a challenge to curriculum silos

Every question in the map carries disciplinary annotations — the fields of knowledge it touches. The map states why (question-map.md, Tier 1):

> "Each question is a node. The disciplines listed beneath it show which fields of knowledge the question touches — not as a curriculum exercise, but as evidence that self-organised curiosity naturally crosses every boundary that formal education erects."

And, in the map's own framing:

> "This document is also a challenge to the siloed structure of the school curriculum. A 9-year-old asking 'can water get sick?' is simultaneously doing environmental law, microbiology, philosophy of rights, and political economy. The map makes that visible." (question-map.md, Tier 1)

Look at the annotations already logged for the seed questions: "Who owns the water?" spans property law, commons theory, political philosophy, environmental economics, indigenous rights and UK water privatisation history; "Could we live without water?" spans biology, survival science, climate science, geopolitics, food systems and philosophy of necessity (question-map.md, Tier 1). No timetable puts those in one lesson. A child's question does.

**What this means for you as facilitator:** the annotations are secondary — "The questions are primary sources. The annotations are secondary." (question-map.md, Tier 1). Capture the question verbatim first; annotate honestly afterwards, listing every discipline the question genuinely touches, including ones you would never find on a primary-school timetable. Do not trim the list to look sensible. The breadth *is* the evidence. (Entry format and mechanics: **iow-pilot-documentation-standards**.)

## 6. Designing good big questions for the water theme

*Facilitation practice — criteria derived from the SOLE literature (§1) and the design of the existing Tier 1 seed questions. Use when designing the 11–14 opener, the 5–7 opener, or Phase-2 pivot questions.*

A good SOLE big question is:

- **Open** — it has no single correct answer, and an adult expert could not close it in one sentence. ("Who owns the water?" cannot be settled; "What is the chemical formula of water?" can.)
- **Emotionally graspable** — a child of the target age can *feel* the question before they can research it. Sickness, survival, fairness, ownership — these land at every age.
- **Cross-disciplinary by nature** — answering it properly drags you across law, science, history, ethics, economics. If it sits comfortably inside one school subject, it is too small.
- **Genuinely unknown to the facilitator** — the best big questions are ones nobody in the room can fully answer. This makes the not-answering rule (§2) easy to keep.
- **Locally alive but not locally closed** — the water theme works on the Isle of Wight because the island's coastline "faces ongoing sewage discharge from water companies" (README.md, Tier 1); the question should let children reach that reality themselves, without naming it for them.

Avoid:

- **Closed factual questions** — anything answerable by the first search result ("How much of the Earth is covered by water?"). Groups finish in four minutes and the self-organisation never starts.
- **Curriculum-aligned phrasing** — "Describe the water cycle" is a worksheet, not a wonder. If a question could appear in a SAT paper, rewrite it or discard it.
- **Leading toward Pooseidon** — do not design questions that funnel children toward buoys, sensors, monitoring networks or "alerting water companies". Pooseidon is the founder's pre-session concept (README.md, Tier 1; Phase 1 discovery brief); the children's direction must be theirs. A question like "How could we detect pollution in the sea?" fails this test. "Who owns the water?" passes: it opens the same territory without prescribing the destination.
- **Questions with a hidden right answer you are hoping for** — that is a predetermined answer wearing a costume, and it violates non-negotiable 1.

**Background colour, clearly fenced:** the project's early exploratory chats (Tier 3 — unconfirmed, Feb–May 2025) collected published SOLE-effectiveness research (critical thinking, metacognition, learner independence across many countries) and sketched ideas for combining SOLEs with hackspaces for island climate adaptation, including mobile SOLE/hackspace units ("SOLE Hackspace Integration Ideas.md"; "Island Network for Climate Action.md", Archive\RAW). Treat these as the design-era thinking that fed the pilot, not as pilot facts: the mobile-unit line is DORMANT (Phase 1 discovery, 2026-07-03) and the settled, current design is in-school SOLEs on the Isle of Wight (Tier 1).

## 7. Quick pre-session self-check (theory side)

Logistics checklists live in **iow-pilot-session-operations**; this is the theory check.

- [ ] I can state the opening question for this band verbatim, and it is the Tier 1 designed opener (or a newly designed one already logged in question-map.md before today).
- [ ] I am not carrying "Can water get sick?" as an opener for 5–7 — it is the benchmark, and must emerge or not on its own.
- [ ] I know my only interventions are redirection, returned questions, and grandmother-method admiration (§2).
- [ ] I have not mentioned, and will not mention, buoys, sensors, Pooseidon, or any destination.
- [ ] I know that a child's question resembling a seed question gets captured verbatim, not echoed, completed, or rephrased.
- [ ] I have read **iow-pilot-safeguarding-and-ethics** and the current safeguarding position (as of 2026-07-03: nothing formal exists yet — no DBS, no consent forms, no school agreement; sessions cannot run until that buildout is complete. Phase 1 discovery, 2026-07-03).
